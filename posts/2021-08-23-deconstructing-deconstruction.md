Deconstruction is so hot right now.

I started to hear about it when Joshua Harris mentioned [on instagram](https://www.instagram.com/p/B0ZBrNLH2sl/?hl=en) that he was "deconstructing" his faith, and was no longer a Christian:

> *The information that was left out of our announcement is that I have undergone a massive shift in regard to my faith in Jesus. The popular phrase for this is “deconstruction,” the biblical phrase is “falling away.” By all the measurements that I have for defining a Christian, I am not a Christian.*

Since then I've heard of friends and acquaintances that are undergoing the process of "deconstruction". But what does that even mean?

According to a [Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstruction) on the topic,  "*Deconstruction argues that language, especially in ideal concepts such as truth and justice, is irreducibly complex, unstable, or impossible to determine.*"

To the people (like Harris) that use the term, they seem to imply that they're undergoing a serious reconsideration of values and beliefs once tightly held and assumed to be true.

At face value, it seems like a very healthy thing. However, the traditional, philosophical method of deconstruction evaluates everything (cultural values, philosophical ideas, personal experiences) as equally legitimate, so as the deconstruction begins, once it gets to the objective truth claims about scripture, that gets thrown out as well.

For many people undergoing "deconstruction", the end is usually a wholesale rejection of the truth of the gospel.

Why? 

- Because the culture values things that are in direct opposition to the Apostle Paul's writings.

- Because today's philosophy says that truth should be seen through a personal and cultural lens, and any truth that has been used to perpetuate oppression should be treated as a lie, regardless of how "true" it is.

- Because people have personally experienced hatred from those who uphold scripture as true, and have experienced love and acceptance from those who say it's false.

## The subversion of deconstruction

Although the popular term is "deconstruction", I take issue with that word. Deconstruction seems implies a careful treatment of something, analyzing the parts to then appreciate it in a wider context. In other words, it makes it seem as though the thing being deconstructed still has value.

According to [Merriam-Webster's post](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/deconstruction) on the word,

> *Deconstruction doesn't actually mean "demolition;" instead it means "breaking down" or analyzing something (especially the words in a work of fiction or nonfiction) to discover its true significance, which is supposedly almost never exactly what the author intended.*

The problem with the term is that it has a subversive intent. It breaks things down to get to a conclusion that the author didn't want. When used on scripture it's pretty much making it say whatever you want. Any value that scripture might hold can now be used as a weapon against others, or as a self-help tool for your own ends.

**You decide good and evil.**

The original temptation.

Deconstruction is the tool of the cynic, the person too sophisticated to [cut out pieces of the Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_Bible) because he doesn't think it fits. Instead he delegitimizes it through competing truth claims and appeals to cultural norms. It appears to be a careful evaluation, but it really begins with a goal in mind: demolition.

## The need for true "deconstruction"

The bible has been around for quite some time, and man-made teachings are built on top of the core teaching. It's been happening for millennia. Jesus accused the Pharisees of this very thing in Matthew 23:4

> *They tie up heavy, cumbersome loads and put them on other people’s shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to lift a finger to move them.*

This is a natural consequence of studying scripture, making inferences, and studying the inferences and making inferences on top of the inferences.

This is how we end up with church splits over esoteric theological points that require 6 pages of preamble to explain completely ("*Infralapsarian heretic!*")

Whether we call it Deconstruction, Dismantling, Foundational Truth Searching, there needs to be a "back-to-basics" look at Christianity in the lives of each person that has received Jesus' gift of salvation. We need to move past our tribal beliefs and understand what are the core things that make us part of Christ's body.

C.S. Lewis, in his preface to "Mere Christianity", describes Christianity like a great house, with the various rooms being denominations and theologies, and the basic unifying beliefs are the hall of the great house.

> * If I can bring anyone into that hall I shall have done what I attempted. But it is in the rooms, not in the hall, that there are fires and chairs and meals. The hall is a place to wait in, a place from which to try the various doors, not a place to live in. For that purpose the worst of the rooms (whichever that may be) is, I think, preferable. It is true that some people may find they have to wait in the hall for a considerable time, while others feel certain almost at once which door they must knock at. I do not know why there is this difference, but I am sure God keeps no one waiting unless He sees that it is good for him to wait.*

It's important for believers to spend some time in the hall of Christianity to understand its foundational value. If there are certain beliefs that derive from inferences of texts, or a certain interpretation, they do not diminish the value of the whole, they're merely structures built in an attempt to better understand the foundation. However, it should never be mistaken as the foundation itself, because when a man-made doctrine is rejected under the assumption that it is essential to Christianity, there is a risk of rejecting the whole thing.

So let's dismantle the additional structures put on by humans that may or may not be right, to discover the solid rock that is the truth of God's word.

But let's not kid ourselves. That honest search for truth isn't deconstruction.
