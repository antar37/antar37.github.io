I've done it. You've probably done it.

You have something really great that you want to put into the world, or some important news that you want to get across, or there's a cause you're passionate about, and you want everyone to be a part.

So you go to a social media platform and craft a beautiful post. It's incredible — with a captivating intro, a moving description, and a heartfelt call to action.

And then, the shares come:

"John shared this".

"Mary shared this"

"Silvia shared this"

The notifications are making your phone sing, and you're thinking, "This is it! We're REACHING PEOPLE!" Your marketing strategy worked perfectly!

But then, you wait for the signups, for the donations, for the customers.

Crickets.

Lots of eyeballs, but no real engagement.

## What happened?

Having someone share something to their own network of followers seems, in theory, to be a great way to get people to learn about your new product or project. "It's the network effect! It's viral!"

Unfortunately it rarely works out that way. Why not? I've got a few theories:

### Virtue Signaling

"Virtue Signaling" is a term that describes the outward actions of someone that is trying show virtuous character. It's not a new concept — here's how Jesus described the virtue signalers of his day:

> And when you pray, you must not be like the hypocrites. For they love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners, that they may be seen by others. Truly, I say to you, they have received their reward.

—Matthew 6:5

Virtue signalling is the reason people want their electric cars to "look" eco-friendly (Prius, anyone?), even though there are plug-ins or hybrids that look exactly like the normal car model.

It's the reason people do video chats in front of their bookcase ("I have many leather-bound books and my apartment smells of rich mahogany.")

It's probably the reason they shared your post.

In other words, it's not really about you. By sharing your content, the sharer was able to communicate something about themselves ("Look, I support small businesses!" "Check it out, I'm socially conscious!" "I want you to think I donated!")

### The Bystander Effect

The Bystander Effect is what happens in videoconfrence meetings when the host asks, "so, what does everyone think? Does anyone have a question?", and there are blank stares all around.

There may be questions or comments, but everyone thinks that someone ELSE will engage. Meanwhile, they'll keep themselves on mute and take another bite of their sandwich.

[https://www.youtube.com/embed/4pQIpZr7xSI](https://www.youtube.com/embed/4pQIpZr7xSI)

The Bystander Effect happens in social media, too. Someone shares content, thinking "surely SOMEONE'S got to do SOMETHING. I'll pass on the info" 

## What to do?

Maybe this has been your experience - how do you put content out into the world in a way that ACTUALLY impacts?

### Make it about selfish generosity

When someone shares your content, they're either doing it to signal their virtue, or they're doing it as a favor to you. It's the reason your mom is the first one to share your stuff, even though she probably has no idea what it's about (hi mom!). 

Compare that with memes. No one cares who originally posted the meme - it's funny! In fact, the person sharing it isn't being generous to the original content creator, he's generously sharing it with his followers. He gets to be the hero in this story.

The question to ask yourself is: Does my content create actual value that will make the sharer seem like a generous person for having shared it?

#### PayPal & selfish generosity

Paypal took this to an [extreme](https://www.referralcandy.com/blog/paypal-referrals/) when they first started. They had a referral program that would pay each person $10 for a referral, and give away $10 to the new signups. Ten bucks for free? This made you look like a hero to all of your friends, while giving some value to the sharer. "Hey everyone, get on PayPal, I've got free money for you!"

So when you're creating the content you want to share, don't think about the people that will share it because they have a personal connection to you — think about how it will be valuable to the person that receives it. Any content that we create and distribute freely is a gift. Make sure that your gift has value.

### Put some skin in the game

Another possiblity is to encourage people not to mindlessly share things, but to share their experiences with others. It's the reason those horrible (but effective) [one-page marketing websites](https://pro.chooseyourselffinancial.com/p/ALR_sidehustle_ar_0219/WALRV703/Full?h=true) have so many testimonials — it's showing people actually using the product, and others to take part.

So if you want to get more people to visit your taco stand, don't just post something and hope others will share. Invite people to share their experience through a photo, a review, a testimonial. Make the message "I've tried it and loved it. I want you to try it too".

### Do it old-school

When getting certified to administer CPR, the trainers warn about the bystander effect. They get around it by suggesting that the person giving CPR point out someone in the crowd, and instead of saying "Someone call 9-1-1!", say "Hey! You in the red shirt and blue hat — call 9-1-1!"

A personal plea is always going to go way, WAY farther than a simple "share". 

So, if you really want to get your content out there, share it, one by one, with people that you think would appreciate it. You'll get more traction that way.

The next time you share something and it flops, don't take it personally. Go back, inject more value into your gift, see how you get people to actually show the value it was to THEM, and reach out to people personally - Go share your gift.
