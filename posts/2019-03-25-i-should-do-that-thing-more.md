I've been told I should write.

I've wanted to work out more.

I've wanted to program more.

I've wanted to do lots of stuff more, but I haven't "found the time".

I'm a terrible procrastinator, and I usually keep things on the backburner until I forget about them. 

However, I've been trying to get better. I'm using a habit tracker to quantify my habits (aiming for that 21-day streak!), and trying to get to a point where I've found the method to do more, and complain less about my not doing.

What's been my strategy?

## Daily goals should be clearly defined tasks that don't take more than ten minutes.

You don't want to get over-enthusiastic and create ten different daily goals that would effectively take several hours to complete... that's a part-time job, not a daily rhythm adjustment.

Keep your goals clearly defined, even though you're looking towards a bigger goal. For example, don't say "lose half a pound a week" as a goal. That's too broad. Something like "walk 10,000 steps" would be better, even though your END goal is to lose weight.

Keep things short. Think of the things that you absolutely HAVE to do every day (brush your teeth, take a shower, etc). They don't take very long. If you've got an everyday goal like "run", keep in mind that from getting ready to coming back from your run will eat up at least 30 minutes of your time. Combine that with 5 or 6 other goals you want to accomplish, and you're looking at 1.5 to 2 hours of daily tasks. 

## Start with things you kind of like, but aren't organized enough to be consistent with.

If you're looking to make your daily goal as natural as brushing your teeth, you should probably start with things you already enjoy doing. Otherwise, you'll quickly get frustrated and give up. Once you feel the VICTORY of turning a few goals into habits, you can move on to things you don't really enjoy. There are two reasons you need to intentionally track the daily goals you've chosen: One, you're a disorganized mess, and two, you lack the willpower to get through unenjoyable tasks. Work on your organization first, then tackle the unenjoyable stuff.

## Only ramp up once you have a 21-day streak

You need to prove to yourself that you can do this. So start slowly, and work your way up to new things. Get a streak in, and then ramp up. If your goal is getting fit, having something like "Do 30 pushups" would be a good goal. If after a while you're feeling ambitious and want to bump it up to 40 or 50, wait until you've got a solid streak at the previous amount (I'm not saying you shouldn't do more if you're up for it, — just don't hold yourself to a DAILY goal at that new, higher amount until you've hit a 21-day streak).

## Don't feel terrible if you fall off the wagon

You're trying to turn activities into habits - activities that aren't currently part of your daily rhythm. That's difficult. Not impossible, but difficult. Don't worry if you mess up or skip a couple of days. You might even need to put that particular habit on hold until you've worked up the willpower to tackle it (forming new habits — any habits — mold and grow your willpower)

## Know your rhythms

Some people want to work out EVERY DAY, but might have weekends that are particularly crazy, or might travel a lot and can't hit some of the goals in a different environment (you're not going to write a page of that novel you've been working on while you're in meetings all day).

So know your rhythm, and anticipate it. Mondays are crazy? Don't go out for your daily walk on Mondays — 6 days a week is fine. Weekends are very different from your weekend rhythm? Adjust — have weekday goals. In the end, it's about giving you the structure you need to get the results you want. A little bit at a time.

This article was written over the course of a week, following a weekday goal of "Write a paragraph".
